# Next GM UI/UX Doctrine

## Purpose

This document is the product and experience north star for Next GM. It describes what the game should feel like, what the interface should help the player believe, and what kind of decisions the experience should make satisfying.

It is not an implementation checklist. It should guide taste, priority, and product judgment without turning every future UI pass into a compliance exercise.

Next GM should feel like a living wrestling broadcast universe that the player is trusted to run.

The player is not filling out forms in a generic management app. The player is running a wrestling brand inside a world of ratings pressure, locker room politics, audience heat, rival promotions, business tradeoffs, creative momentum, and internet chaos.

## The Fantasy

Next GM should make the player feel like four people at once:

- A promotion executive protecting the future of the brand.
- A TV producer shaping a weekly wrestling show.
- A talent evaluator spotting who can carry the company.
- A wrestling fan chasing moments that feel hot, dramatic, surprising, and earned.

The UI should carry that fantasy at every level. Even routine screens should feel like they belong inside a premium wrestling management game, not an admin product with wrestling labels attached.

The emotional blend is:

- Premium dark sports broadcast.
- Underground wrestling command center.
- Franchise-mode clarity.
- Draft-night tension.
- Dirt-sheet and social-media flavor.
- GM office decision pressure.

Next GM is not a spreadsheet simulator, a SaaS dashboard, a passive booking form, or a chatbot wrapped around a wrestling database. It is a game about judgment.

## Experience Principles

### 1. Make The Player Feel In Charge

The player should always feel like the final decision-maker.

The UI can advise, warn, summarize, compare, and dramatize. It can help the player understand stakes. It can make consequences legible. But it should not quietly take creative control away from the player.

When the game recommends something, the recommendation should feel like staff input. When the game warns about something, the warning should feel like operational awareness. When the game resolves an outcome, the outcome should feel like the world responding to the player's choices.

### 2. Treat Wrestling Logic As Real Logic

Next GM should respect the strange, emotional logic of wrestling.

Momentum, heat, title scenes, overexposure, morale, fan reaction, dirt-sheet narratives, locker-room tension, and breakout moments should feel as real as money, ratings, and contracts.

The UI should not flatten everything into accounting math. It should help the player read the promotion the way a wrestling GM would: who feels hot, who needs protection, who has unfinished business, what story has juice, what audience sentiment is turning, and what risk is worth taking.

### 3. Clarity First, Depth On Demand

The player should understand the most important thing on a screen almost immediately.

Depth belongs where it helps decision-making: comparisons, tabs, drawers, profiles, scouting reports, expanded cards, and focused detail views. The default experience should be curated and readable, not a wall of equal-weight information.

Dense screens are good when comparison matters. Clean screens are better when momentum, drama, or orientation matters.

### 4. Big Moments Deserve Staging

Not every screen should shout. But the game should know when something matters.

Draft night, title changes, PLEs, major rivalry turns, breakout performances, big social reactions, shocking injuries, contract drama, and brand-defining wins should get stronger presentation than everyday management.

The game should occasionally feel like a broadcast package snapped into place around the player's choices.

### 5. Consequences Come After Action

The booking experience should preserve tension.

Before a show runs, the player should see operational risks and creative context, not predicted grades, fan reaction, social buzz, rivalry movement, morale changes, or business impact.

After the show runs, the game can reveal what happened. That reveal is part of the fun. Creative feedback should feel retrospective, not predictive.

### 6. The Interface Should Feel Like A Game Hub

Next GM should feel like a premium management game, not an enterprise admin tool.

Navigation should feel compact, cinematic, and spatial. The player should move through the game like they are moving through modes in a sports franchise title: Dashboard, Booking, Roster, Scouting, Calendar, Management, Settings.

The shell should support the fantasy without fighting the content. It should stay lightweight, readable, and confident.

## Visual North Star

Next GM should read as a serious premium wrestling GM management game.

The visual language should blend:

- WWE 2K-style mode familiarity.
- ESPN and NFL Draft broadcast polish.
- Sports franchise menu clarity.
- Dark backstage production-room atmosphere.
- Underground wrestling venue mood.

The default mood is **Premium Dark Broadcast meets Underground Wrestling**: dark, polished, readable, tactical, and management-focused.

Everyday management screens should feel like a night-time production room, scouting cockpit, or GM command center. Major moments should feel more theatrical: draft board graphics, arena lighting, promo-package energy, PLE posters, social fallout, and broadcast recaps.

The UI should favor sharp sports-broadcast rectangles, clean panel structure, tactical glass, controlled glow, brand-led lighting, LED texture, titantron energy, lower-third language, and strong hierarchy.

Avoid visual choices that make the game feel like a generic web app: bubbly cards, random blob decoration, soft SaaS dashboards, oversized rounded panels, pill overload, scattered accent colors, and endless card walls.

Brand color should create identity and atmosphere, not flood the entire screen. Raw, SmackDown, NXT, and AEW-inspired palettes should feel different in lighting, accents, selected states, and hero treatment while sharing one coherent product system.

## Core Player Journey

The player journey should begin with a title-screen fantasy, not a database state.

The opening flow should feel like entering a wrestling management game:

1. Title screen.
2. Continue or select save.
3. Start a new game.
4. Sign the contract.
5. Set up the new GM experience.
6. Choose a GM identity.
7. Choose a brand fantasy.
8. Enter draft night.
9. Review the draft.
10. Land on the Week 1 Brand Dashboard.

The opening experience should sell aspiration before administration. Starting a new game should feel like being hired, trusted, and thrown into pressure.

Save selection should feel like a clean sports franchise save surface. It should help the player recognize their worlds without pretending to be the main dashboard.

## Dashboard

The Brand Dashboard is home base.

It should answer:

> Here's your brand. Here's this week's show. Here's what could go wrong.

The dashboard should feel cinematic but useful: a command-center view of the current week, brand health, key pressure points, and the next important action.

The primary action should usually be to book or continue the current week's show. Alerts should feel like game objectives and pressure points, not an executive memo.

The dashboard should be curated. It should not become a customizable analytics wall by default. The player should feel oriented, not buried.

## Booking

Booking should feel like a hybrid production timeline.

The player should feel like both a GM and a TV producer: building an opening segment, matches, promos, backstage beats, pacing, runtime, and the main event.

The screen should make creative assembly feel visual and tactile without becoming fiddly. Drag-and-drop can be satisfying for rearranging a card, but core actions should also work without drag interactions.

Booking should support decisions, not spoil outcomes. It can show availability, conflicts, champions, rivalries, fatigue, morale pressure, and context. It should not reveal simulated creative results before the show runs.

## Show Results And Week Review

Show Results should feel like a broadcast recap plus a consequence screen.

The first read should focus on the big story:

- How the show landed.
- What the best moment was.
- Who gained or lost momentum.
- What the crowd cared about.
- What changed for the brand.

Detailed segment feedback can follow, but the top of the screen should feel like a recap package rather than a spreadsheet.

Week Review should complete the loop:

Book Show -> Run Show -> Show Results -> Week Review -> Advance Week

The review should connect creative, roster, rivalry, social, and business consequences so the player feels the week mattered.

## Draft And Scouting

Draft night should feel like a sports broadcast sitting on top of a GM war room.

The player should see best available talent, roster needs, pick order, rival brand movement, and scouting uncertainty. Draft decisions should expose traits, upside, fit, and risk rather than reducing every prospect to exact hidden math.

Picking someone should feel dramatic but quick. The moment should be staged, confirmed, and then the draft should keep moving.

Scouting should preserve uncertainty. It should help the player form beliefs, not hand them perfect knowledge.

## Roster, Rivalries, And Championships

The roster should feel like a living locker room, not just a table.

The player should be able to read who matters, who is rising, who is frustrated, who needs TV time, who is overexposed, who is hurt, who has heat, and who belongs in each title scene.

Roster cards should carry character and status. Tables should exist for deeper management and comparison.

Championships should feel prestigious. A title screen should spotlight the champion, the reign, the contenders, the current story, and the health of the division.

Rivalries should feel elastic and alive. They can be started manually, shaped through booking, and emerge from the world over time. The UI should help organize them without making them feel artificially capped or over-systematized.

## Social And IWC

The social layer should make the world feel alive after shows happen.

Before the first show, there is no buzz. After the first show, the internet should start reacting.

The IWC/social experience should feel spicy, funny, emotional, analytical, conspiratorial, and occasionally harsh without becoming unpleasant to use.

It should include fan posts, dirt-sheet rumors, analyst takes, fantasy booking, viral moments, star-rating debates, title-scene arguments, push complaints, memes, and overreactions.

The social layer should reflect outcomes. It should not invent gameplay truth separate from the simulation.

## Finance And Analytics

Finance should be clear, gamey, and decision-focused.

It should help the player understand budget, revenue, expenses, profit/loss, tickets, merch, media revenue, production costs, trends, and financial pressure without becoming an accounting simulator.

Analytics should explain performance drivers:

- Which wrestlers move merch.
- Which rivalries create buzz.
- Which shows convert attention into attendance.
- Which brand is growing faster.
- How ratings, social heat, attendance, and revenue connect.

Finance reports what happened. Analytics helps explain why.

## Calendar And Season Flow

The calendar should orient the player on the Road to PLE.

It should show weekly shows, fixed PLEs, go-home weeks, current timing, and major structural milestones. It should help the player feel the season moving.

The calendar should not become a dumping ground for every kind of alert. Contract issues, injuries, scouting details, rivalry status, finance warnings, and booking readiness belong in more focused places.

## Assistant Philosophy

The assistant should feel like staff, not autopilot.

Assistant modes can eventually include Assistant GM, Creative, Analyst, Scout, and Producer. Each mode should feel like a different staff perspective, not one generic chatbot.

Recommendations should be ask-first. Suggestions should be editable, reviewable, and clearly labeled. The player should always have final approval.

The assistant can eventually phrase, explain, summarize, and brainstorm. It should not secretly decide strategy, invent outcomes, mutate state, or take creative control away from the player.

## Usability Promises

Next GM should be laptop-first and playable on common 11-13 inch screens without feeling cramped or broken.

The experience should be:

- Readable at a glance.
- Keyboard-aware.
- Clear without relying only on color.
- Respectful of reduced-motion needs.
- Usable without drag-and-drop as the only path.
- Dense only where density helps.

The interface should never make the player fight text overflow, clipped controls, hidden critical actions, or unclear status.

## Product Taste Checks

When making UI decisions, ask:

- Does this feel like a wrestling management game or a generic dashboard?
- Does this make the player feel more like the GM?
- Is the most important decision obvious?
- Is the drama saved for moments that deserve it?
- Is the game preserving uncertainty where uncertainty is fun?
- Is the UI explaining the world without over-explaining the math?
- Does this screen help the player decide what to do next?
- Would this still feel good after the player has used it for 50 in-game weeks?

## What This Doctrine Should Protect

This doctrine should protect the soul of Next GM:

- A serious premium wrestling GM fantasy.
- Player agency.
- Broadcast energy.
- Clear decisions.
- Living-world feedback.
- Retrospective consequences.
- Tasteful density.
- Brand atmosphere.
- Game-first navigation and presentation.

It should not become a wall of prohibitions. When a future idea serves the fantasy, improves clarity, or gives the player a better GM story, it belongs in the conversation.
